# KOLO Whitepaper

A participatory common-good system

Published by: KOLO Foundation, Sombor · ekolo.rs

# KOLO in brief

KOLO is a participatory common-good system: a community of people who exchange goods, services and knowledge and together keep a ledger of who contributed to what. That ledger is not money and is not converted into money. The closest analogy is an entry in a civil register: it records a fact, but is not in itself a means that can be spent outside the system.

The system has three parts. The common good (the software, the rules, the contribution ledger and the content created in the system) belongs to no one individually, neither to the founder nor to the Foundation. The KOLO Protocol is the software that keeps the ledger and applies the rules, automatically and without discretion. The KOLO Foundation is a legal entity seated in Sombor that safeguards the common good and is not its owner: it provides the infrastructure, receives donations and represents the system in legal transactions. Money enters only the Foundation, and POEN exists only in the Protocol's ledger.

The Protocol keeps two units. POEN records contribution and exchange: new records arise only through the contribution channels established by the acts, and in an exchange an existing record is only re-registered from one user to another, so the total number does not change. ZRNO records a participant's position in the common good; from activated ZRNO arises a vote in deciding on the rules, and voting power grows with the square root of the number of active ZRNO, not in proportion to it. Exchange and governance are thereby separated.

Who is who in the system is established by people who know each other. The reality of a new user is confirmed by a Kolo member who knows them personally, or who introduces them into the Kolo after a telephone conversation; identity documents are not collected. The Kolo is the circle of participants, those who have been confirmed or introduced into the Kolo in the chain of confirmations. The Kolo is not a legal entity, and belonging to the Kolo is not membership in the Foundation.

Governance is gradually transferred to the community. In the first phase the rules of the system are established by the Management Board of the Foundation. When the total number of recorded POEN reaches the threshold set by the Rulebook on the KOLO System, the Upper Kolo is established, a body made up of Kolo keepers with activated ZRNO. The Upper Kolo establishes proposals on the rules, and the Management Board adopts them by its own act.

The legal nature of the system rests on four principles: non-convertibility, absence of property rights over records, non-refundability of donations and automatic recording. Alongside them stand two limits of the common good: data minimisation and open licences (software under AGPL-3.0, content under CC BY-SA 4.0). Neither the principles nor the limits are changed by any governance decision.

This document explains why the system is designed this way. The rules, amounts, time limits and procedures are found in the acts of the KOLO system, which are listed in the Rulebook on the Hierarchy of Acts of the KOLO System. The Whitepaper is not a binding act; in case of discrepancy, the act prevails. Terms used in this document in the masculine gender apply equally to all persons.

# A day in Sombor

The story that follows is invented. The people in it do not exist, but everything they do is possible under the rules of the system.

## Morning

Ana sews. On the Pijaca (Marketplace), the part of the platform intended for listings, she has posted that she shortens trousers and mends clothes. She set the amount stated in the listing herself; the Foundation does not set it, does not recommend it and does not publish any ratio of POEN to the dinar. Her neighbour Vesna brought her three pairs of trousers, and when the work was done, she re-registered POEN to Ana from her own record. Not a single dinar moved. Vesna's record is smaller, Ana's larger, and the total number of POEN in the system is the same. The same morning Vesna also posted a listing looking for someone to paint her fence, and in the Chat Room she asked her neighbours who had a ladder to lend.

Ana is in the Kolo because she was confirmed by the teacher Jelica, who has known her since childhood and who is herself a Kolo member. Ana has three children and has applied to the Mothers Support programme. Before the Foundation's decision, Jelica confirmed that Ana meets the condition; the data entered about the children were not shown to her. Since the application was approved, the Protocol records a contribution under the programme for Ana every day, within the daily limit that applies to the whole system.

Ana's son is ten years old and has an account in the children's space. He is a Kolo child, because Ana is a Kolo member and because Jelica, who knows the whole family, confirmed that Ana has that son. He makes friendships only in person, when another child scans his code. He does not talk or exchange with adult users. When he wanted to re-register more POEN than usual to a friend, the re-registration waited for Ana to approve it. This morning, in the Wish Box, he proposed a sticker-swapping game and supported other children's proposal for a drawing competition; the Foundation reviews proposals before other children see them, and no one sees the names of the authors. He has also entered his school, so he sees the other Kolo children from it; adults do not see that list. Ana sees the list of his friends and the text of his proposals, but does not read his conversations with other children.

## Noon

A month ago Dragan moved from Novi Sad and did not know anyone in Sombor. He created an account, entered his name, place and telephone number and asked for someone to call him. Jelica called him. Before she opened his number, she gave a statement of confidentiality, and the Foundation recorded that she had opened the call. They talked for half an hour, and Jelica introduced him into the Kolo; since then Dragan has been a member without confirmation, until someone who gets to know him confirms him. One of the founders, as a Kolo keeper, later reviewed that introduction, as every introduction from the list is reviewed.

The same day Dragan posted his first listing: he repairs computers. The Foundation reviewed and approved the listing, because it was a genuine offer, so the Protocol recorded for Dragan the contribution for a first listing. Only then, when Dragan had made his first confirmed contribution, did the Protocol record for both him and Jelica the same, one-time contribution for introduction into the Kolo. The person who had once confirmed Jelica received nothing on that basis. A few weeks later Ana, whose computer Dragan had repaired, gave him a recommendation; his profile shows only the number of recommendations, and no one sees who gave them.

Stevan is retired and repairs bicycles in his yard. He was introduced into the Kolo by a neighbour, a member without confirmation, and was later confirmed by Stevan's best man, who is a Kolo member. Stevan is enrolled in the Elderly Support programme. When he needs bread, he takes a voucher issued by Jovana Mitrović's bakery in his neighbourhood.

The bakery is a sponsor. It is entered in the sponsor register and has concluded an agreement with the Foundation by which it undertakes to hand over a specified number of loaves of bread to participants who take vouchers. The Foundation does not receive or possess the bread. The voucher is issued in Stevan's name and for a precisely specified good at that bakery, and Stevan has his own right against the bakery. When Stevan takes a voucher, a number of POEN derived from the inscription under the agreement is annulled in his record; the bakery does not receive POEN. The sponsorship contribution was recorded in the record of the user who submitted the application on behalf of the bakery. The bakery's name and logo appear on the sponsor ranking list and on the vouchers. That is a record, not advertising: the sponsor acquires no right to promotion or advantage in any procedure of the Foundation.

## Afternoon

The Kovač family has a homestead on the edge of Sombor: eggs, honey and vegetables. Marija Kovač is the holder of the farm. They applied for the farm, the Foundation talked with Marija before approval, and the farm received a point on the map of local producers, and its listings the DOMAĆE mark. The mark says only that the listing was posted on behalf of a farm that has joined the system; whoever offers the eggs is responsible for their origin and quality. The Kovač farm is not entered in the register of agricultural holdings, and that is no obstacle: entered and non-entered farms participate under the same conditions. Last week Marija's son, who is a Kolo member, also joined the farm, so a farm contribution is noted for him too, and it is recorded when he posts his own offer.

Marija, who is a Kolo member, and her son are also cooperative members. When there were enough participants in Sombor, people from the Kolo founded a cooperative and entered it in the register of cooperatives; the Foundation helped with the founding, but has no vote in the cooperative's organs. The cooperative's record in the Protocol was opened when it had five cooperative members, and when the tenth joined, the Protocol recorded a new growth threshold in its record, once and for all.

The cooperative keeps a shared cold store and itself determines how many POEN are re-registered to the cooperative's record for its use. The Kovačs brought surplus peppers for distribution: on receipt the cooperative re-registered POEN to them from its record, and the cooperative members who took the peppers re-registered POEN to the cooperative. The distribution was organised by their neighbour Ljiljana, a cooperative member. That is an organisational unit: the cooperative itself decided to assign it to Ljiljana, within the highest daily amount it is entitled to according to the number of cooperative members. When Kolo keepers confirmed that the distribution had taken place, the Protocol recorded an operational contribution for Ljiljana, within the common daily limit. This autumn the cooperative also posted a task for the place, cleaning the canal along the homesteads. When it confirmed completion, it re-registered POEN from its record to those who had worked; unlike the garden by the school, not a single new POEN arose there. Re-registrations on behalf of the cooperative are entered by a neighbour whom the cooperative proposed and the Foundation authorised: he sees the names and telephone numbers of the cooperative members of his own cooperative and nothing more, and does not re-register to himself from the cooperative's record. All participants see the cooperative's re-registrations under its name, and the cooperative members under their pseudonyms.

The cooperative is also a place where people help. Stevan does not like the telephone, so at the cooperative a neighbour showed him how to re-register POEN; the account remained his and no one acts in his place. When Stevan and a neighbour did not agree about a bicycle repair, they turned to the cooperative, not to a court.

This week the community garden by the school was being laid out. The Foundation, on behalf of the community, posted a task divided into units: one unit is one prepared bed. Stevan and Dragan signed up, and after the work they stated only how many beds they had prepared. Completion was confirmed by Kolo keepers, and the Protocol recorded their contribution within the common daily limit.

The same garden needed composters, and no one on the Pijaca offered them. So the Foundation bought them with money from donations, in a collective procurement. The participants who had signed up collected one each; on collection a set number of POEN was annulled for them, which returned to the Protocol, not to the Foundation. How much the composters cost in dinars was published separately, in the report on the use of funds.

Money for that procurement also came from Branko, a retired doctor, who paid a donation to the Foundation from his account. He chose for the donation to be public, so his name, the amount and the date are visible on the list of donations; he can withdraw his name whenever he wants. The donation agreement does not contain a number of POEN; the Protocol, independently of the agreement, recorded his contribution under public rules. A car repair shop on Kralja Petra Street paid money to the Foundation as a sponsor; the contribution was recorded for the owner who submitted the application, not for the shop, and the shop's name appears on the sponsor ranking list.

Milica is a student and is at home at weekends. She gives maths lessons to children from the neighbourhood, and the parents re-register POEN to her for it. She is enrolled in the Student Support programme; she proved her status by a statement, without a certificate of enrolment.

After a fire Zoran was left without a house and now lives with his sister. He is enrolled in the Special Support programme. In the ledger participants see only the pseudonym, the name of the programme and the amount; why Zoran is in the programme no one sees except the Foundation.

## Evening

In the evening Vesna opened the system page. She saw how many people are active, how many listings and exchanges there are, that the sum of all records still amounts to zero, and how many exchanges took place among people who are not founders, because that is the most important measure that the community is alive.

At the end of the day no one in Sombor received or gave a dinar for an exchange through the system. Only the ledger of who gave what to whom changed, and what was exchanged stayed in the town.

When one day the total recognised contribution reaches the threshold from the Rulebook, the system will move into the second phase by itself. Jelica will then be able to inscribe ZRNO from her contribution, and if she activates it, she will have a vote in the Upper Kolo, where the rules are decided by square-root voting.

When there are enough people in the Kolo in Bezdan, a cooperative will arise there too. Honey from Sombor will then be able to go to Bezdan as well, from cooperative to cooperative, and transport between places will be arranged by inter-cooperative logistics.

# 1. Problem

Communities that wish to organise their own exchange face three problems that none of the models described in this chapter solves simultaneously: scaling, trust and the regulatory framework.

Scaling. Direct exchange, barter, works when two people have what the other needs, at the same time and in the same place, and that condition is rarely met (Jevons, 1875). Time banks solve the problem of simultaneous need by recording hours, but in their basic form they assume that every hour is of equal value, which limits the complexity of exchange (Cahn, 2004). LETS systems allow more flexible exchange within a closed group; they spread quickly, but exchange in them remained small and limited to a narrower circle of people (Leyshon et al., 2001). Regional currencies (Chiemgauer, the Bristol Pound) are issued for money, at par, and redeemed for a fee. In mutual credit networks (WIR, Sardex) the unit arises through credit, and the negative is in the end collected in money. None of these models meets all the requirements; rather, they complement one another (Seyfang, 2006).

Trust. Every exchange system requires people to trust that their contribution will be recognised and that they will not be exploited. In a small group trust is built face to face; when the system grows, a mechanism is needed that replaces knowing every member (cf. Luhmann, 1979, on the difference between personal and system trust). That role is traditionally taken by the state, through regulation, or by the market, through price and contract. A community that wants its own exchange needs a third way: rules that are at the same time public, predictable and applied without the discretion of an individual.

Regulatory framework. Even when it solves scaling and trust, a community faces law written for financial instruments, payment services and digital assets. Every internal record that resembles money risks a classification that requires a licence and supervision intended for other activities. Complementary systems have had different outcomes. WIR in Switzerland has operated as a bank since 1936. Wörgl was stopped by the national bank in 1933. The Bristol Pound was wound up in 2020–2021, when the costs of the payment infrastructure could not be covered. Banco Palmas in Brazil went through court proceedings with the central bank before it was recognised. A community that wants to organise exchange must know from the first day how its system will be classified, and not after the fact, but as part of the design.

These three problems are interdependent. A system that scales without trust falls apart as soon as it outgrows the local group. A system with trust but without scaling remains a local initiative. A system that has both, but does not take account of the legal framework, gets stopped.

Attempts to solve this have a long history. Silvio Gesell proposed money with a holding cost, to encourage circulation instead of accumulation (Gesell, 1916). Thomas Greco systematised the principles of mutual credit and complementary currencies (Greco, 2009). The neo-mutualist tradition, which joins classical mutualism with contemporary cooperative and digital tools, sought models in which users govern the system they use (Carson, 2007); KOLO is related to that tradition. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can govern common goods without privatisation and without state control, if they have clear rules of access, contribution and decision-making (Ostrom, 1990). Yochai Benkler described production based on common resources and voluntary contribution (Benkler, 2006); platform cooperativism carries cooperative principles over to digital platforms (Scholz, 2016); open cooperativism joins open protocols, cooperative structures and the common good (Bauwens, Kostakis and Pazaitis, 2019). Sensorica developed open contribution accounting (Pazaitis, 2020), and Enspiral uses a foundation as the legal framework of shared infrastructure (Enspiral Handbook).

Each of these models solves part of the problem. Mutual credit scales with difficulty outside special institutional conditions. Commons-based production solves the legal form after the fact, when the project already exists. Ostrom's principles describe the conditions, but do not offer an implementation for a digital system. Platform cooperatives solve ownership, but not the ledger of contribution. Open contribution accounting systems have no legal form that answers the question of regulatory classification in advance.

Meanwhile, international institutions have recognised the social and solidarity economy as a legitimate direction of development (Appendix A). For Serbia, which is aligning its regulations with the law of the European Union, that is the direction in which its regulatory environment is moving. KOLO solves all three problems in one system, with the contribution ledger as the central mechanism.

# 2. Starting point: a common good with rules

To the question of how to organise what matters to many people there are three known answers. Private property gives control to the owner, and access to others on the owner's terms; applied to exchange, it yields a platform whose owner profits from the relationships that users create. State property ensures access, but introduces distance between the user and the decision. Open access without rules leads to what Garrett Hardin called the tragedy of the commons (Hardin, 1968).

Ostrom showed that Hardin's conclusion is not correct: communities have governed common goods for centuries, provided that they have clear rules: clear boundaries, rules adapted to the place, collective decision-making, monitoring and graduated sanctions. A common good does not decay because it is common, but when it has no structure.

KOLO starts from that insight. In the KOLO system the common good is not a pasture or a fishpond, but the system itself: the software, the rules, the contribution ledger and the content. Unlike a pasture, it is predominantly non-rival, because use by one does not reduce availability to others (Hess and Ostrom, 2007), and with the number of people the supply, the choice and the number of possible exchanges grow (Katz and Shapiro, 1985).

A non-rival common good has its own problem: who maintains it, who pays for the infrastructure and who decides. Open-source projects that have survived developed structures of governance and financing, but as a rule after the fact, as a response to crises. KOLO answers that question with a ledger: the system records who contributes, how much and in what way. That ledger is not the property of users (a user has no property right over the record of their contribution), but it allows contribution to be recognised and participation in governance to be based on it.

This is how KOLO differs from related models. No one owns the system: neither the founder, nor the Foundation, nor the users. The system does not depend on a budget or on political will: the Foundation is financed by the community's donations, and the Foundation spends the funds on the objectives in the Statute. Unlike an open-source project, KOLO records every form of contribution, not only code. Unlike crypto projects, there is no token that is traded, no promise of return and no market on which to speculate. Unlike a platform cooperative, KOLO is not a platform that sells a service to users: exchange takes place between users, and the only relationship with the external economy goes through the Foundation, in dinars, and through sponsorship.

A common good with structure: a system in which position is neither altruistic nor speculative, but recorded; in which contribution is not invisible, but is not ownership either; and which promises no one a return.

# 3. What is distinctive in the KOLO system

Contribution without debt. In LETS systems and mutual credit networks exchange begins with indebtedness: whoever receives goes into the negative and owes the community. In the KOLO system no one enters exchange indebted. POEN arises only from contribution that the acts recognise, and the only record that is permanently negative is the Protocol's record, which carries the counter-entry of everything recorded for users. That counter-entry is no one's debt: there is no person against whom anyone would have a claim, the Foundation owes nothing to the holders of records, and no money stands behind the record (chapter 11). The sum serves only to show that no record arose outside the channels. A user can have a negative record only under the closed list of grounds in the Rulebook on the KOLO System, as a correction of the ledger and not as credit, and even then it is not a debt that is collected. In a mutual credit network a member who leaves in the negative leaves a loss to the others. In the KOLO system a negative record stays with that user's history and is not transferred either to the Protocol or to others, so no one bears another's negative.

Two units. Exchange is recorded in POEN, and participation in governance arises from ZRNO. ZRNO is not bought and is not transferred, and voting power grows with the square root of the number of active ZRNO. Voting power arises from activated ZRNO, and not from the number of POEN or from donations; whoever wants a vote must activate ZRNO and thereby gives up the possibility of writing it off.

Proof of reality without documents. That a real person stands behind an account is confirmed by a person who knows them personally, not by a copy of an identity card. Whoever knows no one can get onto the call list, from which a Kolo member calls them in order to get acquainted.

Money only into the Foundation. Dinars enter the Foundation as non-refundable donations and are spent on infrastructure, the operation of the system and community projects. They do not enter the accounting framework: there is no conversion of dinars into POEN or of POEN into dinars.

Local supply. Farms, both entered and not entered in the register, offer food and agricultural products under the DOMAĆE mark and are on the map of local producers. Registered cooperatives coordinate exchange and distribution in their own place.

Sponsorship through vouchers. A company, an association or a registered agricultural holding can support the community by an agreement by which it undertakes to hand over goods or provide services to participants. The Foundation does not possess those goods; it is an agreement for the benefit of third parties, and the holder of a voucher has their own right against the giver.

Children's space. Children from the age of seven participate in a separate space, with a parent and with restrictions according to age.

Support programmes. For mothers, the elderly, persons with reduced capacity or without a home, and students, the Protocol records every day a contribution by which participation that is not visible through individual exchanges is recognised.

Progressive decentralisation, open code and expansion. Governance passes to the community at a threshold known in advance, and the software and content are open. The system is not replicated but expands: a new environment enters the same system through membership and its own cooperative (chapter 13).

# 4. System architecture

## The common good

The common good consists of the software of the system, the rules of the system, the contribution ledger and the content created in the system. It is the collective good of all users, but not collective ownership in the sense of property law: no one has a right of disposal over it or a right to a share in it. The users' relationship to the common good is participatory: a right of use and contribution, not a right of disposal.

The infrastructure (servers, databases and network equipment) is not part of the common good, but its technical precondition. The ledger as a record belongs to the common good, and the database as a medium belongs to the infrastructure. Providing the infrastructure is a service obligation of the Foundation.

## The Protocol

The KOLO Protocol is the software that performs four functions. It keeps a ledger of every activity that the acts provide for. It calculates the accounting coefficient from the state of the ledger. It applies the rules: when the conditions are met, it carries out inscription, re-registration and write-off. It preserves integrity: the sum of all records is always zero, no record arises outside an established channel, and an error is corrected by a counter-entry, not by rewriting history. That is a property of a centralised ledger that the software keeps consistent, not a property of a distributed network.

The Protocol does not decide which rules apply. The rules are established by people, through the procedures described in chapter 9. The Protocol has no legal personality, does not conclude agreements and is not a data controller.

## The Foundation

The KOLO Foundation is a non-profit legal entity founded under the Law on Endowments and Foundations, seated in Sombor. The common good has no legal personality, so it cannot conclude an agreement or hold an account; that is why the Foundation gives it a legal form.

The functions of the Foundation are service functions: it provides and maintains the infrastructure, receives donations, finances the costs of operating the system and projects, and represents the system in legal transactions. The Foundation does not acquire POEN or ZRNO and does not participate in the accounting framework; its relationship with the system is exclusively in dinars. The Foundation safeguards the common good and is not its owner: it has no right of disposal over it, and the licences prevent the software and content from being closed.

The Foundation has no members, so the community does not elect it. That is why the limits of its action are written in advance:

- the licences prevent the software and content from being closed;
- in the second phase the Management Board adopts the decisions of the Upper Kolo without assessing their expediency, and rejects them only on grounds of law, the Statute or competence;
- the four principles and the limits of the common good cannot be changed even by the Foundation;
- if the Foundation ceases to exist, the ledger and the infrastructure are transferred only to a person that accepts the principles and limits of the common good.

## The Community and the Kolo

The KOLO Community is a descriptive term for all users of the system. It is not a legal entity, has no organs or representatives and does not make decisions as a whole. The Community contributes in two ways: by participation in the system, which leaves a record in the ledger, and by financing the Foundation.

The Kolo is the circle of participants, the users who have been confirmed or introduced into the Kolo in the chain of confirmations. The Kolo is not a legal entity, and belonging to the Kolo is not membership in the Foundation or in any other legal entity.

The relationship between the Foundation and the community is not hierarchical but functional: the community finances the Foundation, the Foundation maintains the infrastructure; the community uses the system and contributes, the Foundation represents it in legal transactions. The financial flow is one-directional and in dinars. When the funds exceed the operating costs, the surplus is directed into projects for the benefit of the community, and never to individual users as a payout.

# 5. POEN and ZRNO

## POEN is a record, not a means

POEN is an internal accounting unit: a record in the Protocol's ledger by which contribution and other forms of participation in the common good are recorded. POEN has no holder. It does not exist as an object that someone holds and hands over, but only as a line in the ledger kept by the Protocol.

It is useful to distinguish a means from a ledger. Money, a token or a gift card have a value that is transferred with them. A civil register, a land-registry sheet or minutes record a fact, and are not themselves a value. POEN belongs to the second kind: it records that a contribution took place, but the record itself is neither a value handed from hand to hand nor a promise of future benefit; re-registration changes the ledger and does not hand over a means. Mutual credit networks have already shown that exchange can run through a ledger, without banknotes and without buying units (Greco, 2009). KOLO is a ledger, but differs from mutual credit in that a record does not arise through indebtedness.

## How POEN arises

New POEN records are inscribed only by the Protocol, and only through the contribution channels established by the Rulebook on the KOLO System: operational contribution, confirmation and introduction into the Kolo, donation, sponsorship, cooperative growth, support programmes, founding contribution, first listing, children's contribution in the children's space and farm contribution. No user can inscribe POEN by themselves, nor can the Foundation inscribe it outside those channels.

A contribution is often first noted, and only later recorded. A noted contribution is a contribution whose ground has arisen, but whose condition for inscription has not yet been met; it is not a POEN record and does not enter the total number. When the Foundation approves something in a channel (a first listing, a farm, receipt of a donation), it establishes that a fact has occurred, and does not decide whether to inscribe something for someone. The number of POEN is determined by the Protocol under the rules of the channel.

Each channel has its own limit. Operational contribution and support programmes share a common daily limit, tied to the total number of recorded POEN. The other channels are limited by their own measure: confirmation and introduction into the Kolo, the first listing and the farm contribution are recorded once per person; donation and sponsorship by the funds actually received, or by the goods agreed; cooperative growth by a closed table of thresholds; the founding contribution by an upper limit set in advance; and the contribution from the children's space is written off upon coming of age. The total number of POEN that arises outside the daily limit is thereby tied to the number of real persons, to the funds received and to limits known in advance.

## Re-registration and exchange

When two users exchange a good or a service, the exchange is recorded by re-registration: the record of the one who gave the good or service increases, and the record of the one who received it decreases by the same amount. The total number of POEN does not change.

Exchange is not a payment transaction. Re-registration does not transfer monetary value and is not a payment; the user does not hand over a means, but initiates a change in the ledger kept by the Protocol. The Foundation and the Protocol do not mediate in exchange and are not liable for the obligations of users. No one is obliged to accept POEN. The amount stated in the listing is set by whoever posts the listing, and the parties may also agree on it; the Foundation does not set, recommend or assess any amount.

A re-registration that does not accompany a real exchange but a sale of POEN for money is prohibited by the Terms of Use and is not an exchange of goods and services.

## The zero-sum rule and the negative record

The sum of all POEN records, including the Protocol's record, amounts to zero at every moment. The Protocol keeps a record that is always negative and represents the counter-entry of all POEN recorded for users and cooperatives; the total number of recorded POEN is equal to the absolute value of that record. Every recorded contribution therefore has an exact counter-entry, and every irregularity is immediately visible as a deviation from zero.

A user cannot have a negative record, except under the closed list of grounds in the Rulebook on the KOLO System. All of those grounds are corrections: someone returns what was recorded for them in connection with a particular ground, for example when a false confirmation or a false introduction into the Kolo is annulled, following a complaint about an exchange that was not fulfilled, or upon termination of a farm. A negative record is not a debt and is not collected; the POEN that come in first fill it, and the exchange of goods and services is not restricted. Anyone whose record goes into the negative is always notified of it.

Why does the record not stop at zero? Because that would be an injustice to the one who kept the POEN. If two people receive the same record on the same ground, and the ground later falls away, the one who has already re-registered the record to someone else must not come off more cheaply than the one who kept it. Stopping at zero is not leniency, but an error in the ledger.

One may ask whether the Protocol is then the one that is “in the negative for everyone”, and thereby the community's debtor. It is not. The Protocol's record is the bookkeeping counterpart of recognised contribution, not an obligation: it has no debtor or creditor, no due date, collection, interest, backing or redemption. It decreases only by annulment of records, when a contribution is used or ceases, and not by repayment, because no money flows back. In mutual credit networks the negative side is distributed among the members who owe. In the KOLO system it exists only as an aggregate record that shows how much contribution has been recognised, and no one owes it.

## Annulment upon use

A POEN record is also annulled when the user uses it to participate in a project of the Foundation: when they collect a share from a collective procurement or take a voucher. That is not consideration for a good: the POEN returns to the Protocol, not to the Foundation, and the Foundation receives nothing on that ground. The total number of recorded POEN is thereby reduced.

## ZRNO: position and vote

ZRNO records a participant's position in the common good. From activated ZRNO arises a vote in the Upper Kolo. The total number of ZRNO is unchangeable; at every moment part of it is inscribed with participants, and part is available for inscription in the Protocol.

ZRNO is inscribed from contribution already recorded in the participant's record, regardless of whether the record arose through a contribution channel or through exchange. The inscription is initiated by the participant, and the Protocol checks the conditions and carries it out; no one approves or rejects it. By inscription a Kolo member becomes a Kolo keeper. A member without confirmation can inscribe ZRNO, but does not activate it until they are confirmed. A user who is not in the Kolo does not inscribe ZRNO. A minor user does not inscribe it until coming of age.

Inscribed ZRNO is free or active. Active ZRNO gives a vote; free ZRNO does not give a vote, but can be written off, that is, returned to the Protocol, with POEN recorded at the accounting coefficient applicable at that time. The same ZRNO cannot at the same time serve for a vote and be available for write-off: the governance function and accounting flexibility are mutually exclusive.

ZRNO is not transferred to another user, neither on its own nor together with an account. There is no market for ZRNO, there is no price of ZRNO and there is no possibility of trading it. When a Kolo keeper's confirmation is annulled, the ZRNO remains inscribed for them, but frozen: they do not activate it, do not write it off and do not vote until they are in the Kolo again. The annulment does not carry over to those whom they confirmed or introduced.

In the first phase ZRNO is not inscribed. The role of Kolo keepers is held by the founders, as initial keepers, by virtue of being founders, and not by inscribed ZRNO. The inscription of ZRNO opens with the transition to the second phase.

## The accounting coefficient

The accounting coefficient is the numerical ratio of the total number of recorded POEN to the number of ZRNO available for inscription. It shows how many POEN are needed to inscribe one ZRNO in a given accounting period. The Protocol calculates it at the end of each accounting period, automatically and without discretion.

The coefficient is moved only by changes in the total number of recorded POEN. When the Protocol inscribes new records through the contribution channels, the numerator grows and the coefficient grows. When recorded POEN are annulled (by termination of a user's status, annulment upon use, write-off in the children's space or another ground under the acts), the numerator falls and the coefficient falls. The coefficient, therefore, is not monotonic and is not predetermined to grow. Exchange between users does not affect it, because it moves existing records.

The inscription and write-off of ZRNO also do not move the coefficient, and that is a consequence of the formula itself, not of a rule that could be changed. On inscription the number of available ZRNO is reduced, but the total number of POEN is reduced in the same proportion, because the POEN used for the inscription return to the Protocol; on write-off both quantities grow in the same ratio. A Kolo keeper cannot move the coefficient in their own favour by their own inscription or write-off.

The sum on which the coefficient depends includes the contribution of everyone, including one's own. No one works to create a benefit for a Kolo keeper, no one sets or promises the coefficient, and from it arises neither profit, nor a payout, nor a claim; ZRNO cannot even be sold.

The coefficient is an administrative figure: it is not a price, because there is no market; it is not an exchange rate, because there is no conversion; it is not an indicator of success, because it does not say whether the system works well. It does not express the value of ZRNO or of POEN outside the system.

Whoever inscribed ZRNO earlier inscribed it with fewer recorded POEN than would be needed later, if the coefficient has grown in the meantime. That is a consequence of the formula, and not an incentive to acquire position: the number of POEN needed for inscription is calculated by the Protocol from the state of the ledger, no decision rewards early participation, and no one promises that this number will be higher later.

Inscription is also limited per accounting period: in one period a participant can use for inscription only a small part of their record. The available ZRNO therefore cannot be taken over abruptly, and later participants retain access.

# 6. People in the system and proof of reality

## Statuses

A user is every person with an account on the platform. An adult user has one of four statuses, in the order in which they are acquired:

- user: registered, but not in the Kolo. Browses the system, posts listings offering or seeking, exchanges and re-registers POEN, writes in the Chat Room and contacts an advertiser about a listing. Their contribution is recorded through the first listing, donation and sponsorship;
- member without confirmation: introduced into the Kolo, but not yet confirmed by a Kolo member who knows them personally. Has the rights of a participant, except those that the acts tie to a Kolo member;
- Kolo member: their reality has been confirmed by a Kolo member who knows them personally. Confirms others, calls users from the call list and gives recommendations;
- Kolo keeper: a Kolo member for whom ZRNO has been inscribed, as well as the founders, who are the initial keepers. Supervises the expansion of the chain of confirmations, and by activating ZRNO participates in the Upper Kolo.

Participants (members without confirmation, Kolo members and Kolo keepers) make up the Kolo. A minor user has none of these statuses; their position is governed by the Rulebook on the Participation of Children. When a right is tied to a status, the acts say so expressly; the transition between statuses occurs under the rules, and not by the decision of an individual.

## The chain of confirmations

The reality of a user is confirmed only in the chain of confirmations. Verification, as the acts call it, is based on personal acquaintance or on a telephone conversation, not on checking documents, and has two forms: confirmation and introduction into the Kolo. Confirmation is given by a Kolo member, or a founder, to a person they know personally; the confirmed person becomes a Kolo member. Introduction into the Kolo is performed by a Kolo member after a telephone conversation with a user from the call list, or by a member without confirmation when introducing an acquaintance; the introduced person becomes a member without confirmation. The difference is not a degree of belonging: a user is in the Kolo or is not. The difference determines only the actions that the acts tie to a Kolo member.

Reality is established by one person, once. Whoever confirms or introduces into the Kolo confirms reality (the person exists as a natural person) and continuity (the account is used by the same person) and declares that they are not aware that this person has another account. They do not guarantee uniqueness (one person, one account), because they know the person, not the person's accounts. That two accounts are accounts of the same person is established by the Foundation, by comparing data and contacting the person, with reasons and a right of complaint. The comparison in itself blocks nothing: members of a household may share the same telephone number, and namesakes the same name.

## The call list

Whoever knows no Kolo member can get onto the call list: when they ask for a conversation themselves, or when the Foundation offers it to them after a public donation, sponsorship or a farm application. The name and number they enter are seen only by the Kolo member who calls them, after a statement of confidentiality, and the Foundation records who opened them; misuse is a breach of the rules. A Kolo member who calls never asks for money, a password or a code from the phone; whoever asks for that is not calling on behalf of the system. The Foundation ensures that everyone on the list is called.

Neither a payment, nor sponsorship, nor a farm application, nor getting onto the list confirms reality. The conversation in itself is not an introduction into the Kolo; a Kolo member who calls a user introduces them into the Kolo when, after the conversation, they are satisfied that the user is a real person and understands the rules of the system. A donation, sponsorship and the contribution recorded on their basis do not depend on the call.

## Member without confirmation and confirmation

A member without confirmation exchanges, acquires POEN records, participates in support programmes, procurements, operational contribution and cooperatives, and inscribes ZRNO. They do not see the call list, do not confirm anyone, do not give recommendations, do not supervise and do not activate ZRNO. They introduce into the Kolo only those whom they know personally.

A member without confirmation is confirmed by a Kolo member who knows them personally, by a Kolo keeper after a telephone conversation, or by the Foundation by a reasoned decision. That confirmation does not add a new step in the chain and does not bring POEN. Whoever becomes a Kolo member can confirm those with whom they are linked in the chain, if they know them personally; confirmation thus spreads step by step, always by the decision of people who know each other, never automatically.

## Supervision, pace and a chain without a loop

The number of confirmations and introductions is not limited; the daily pace is limited.

Supervision is carried out by Kolo keepers. The function arises from the status, without appointment. A supervisor checks a confirmation or introduction afterwards and records the outcome; supervision does not change their effect and does not bring POEN. The signals shown to the supervisor determine only the order of review, not the outcome.

Every participant except the founders was brought into the Kolo by exactly one person, so the chain of confirmations is a tree whose roots are the founders. No one can confirm or introduce anyone from their own ascending line, so no closed loops arise in the chain. The defence against the coordinated creation of false accounts (Douceur, 2002) does not rest on the number of confirmations, but on the personal responsibility of the one who confirms, on the daily pace and supervision, and on the fact that neither confirmation nor introduction in itself brings anything until the new participant contributes.

## Responsibility for confirmation

Whoever confirms or introduces into the Kolo someone they do not know is answerable for it. Whether a confirmation or introduction is false is established by the Foundation, for each person separately. A false confirmation is annulled together with what was recorded on its basis, and the one who gave it bears the consequences with their own record, without an upper limit, and as a rule by exclusion. An honest confirmation stands even when the same person has erred elsewhere, and when they leave the system.

## Founders

Every network of personal confirmations has the problem of the first step: who confirms the first ones. In the KOLO system these are the founders: persons from the founding core of the Foundation and persons whom the Management Board designates with a publicly announced identity. They are the initial Kolo keepers and the root of the chain of confirmations; their confirmed status does not arise from anyone's confirmation. The capacity of founder does not give the powers of the Management Board. All later Kolo keepers acquire the status by the regular route. The circle of founders is closed: no one later becomes a founder, not even by entering the organs of the Foundation.

## Identification in the dinar flow

The dinar flow has separate identification. Donations arrive only by non-cash means, and identification of the payer and verification of the origin of funds are carried out by the bank, which is the obliged entity for that. The Foundation compares the information about the payer with the user's account, in order to record the contribution for the person with whose funds the payment was made. That check serves the dinar flow, not proof of reality.

## Children

Children participate because they are part of the community: in the children's space they learn reciprocity (to give, to receive and to agree) within a framework made according to their age. Every rule of the children's space is measured by the best interests of the child, so protection always comes before the scope of participation.

A minor from the age of seven participates in the children's space, under a special regime. A child's account is in one of three states. A waiting child has an account that a parent has not yet taken over and makes only friendships in person. A child with a parent has a parent who has taken over the account, but is not a Kolo member, or whose child's existence has not yet been confirmed; such a child communicates only with their own friends and does not take part in the re-registration of POEN. A Kolo child has a parent who is a Kolo member and for whom a Kolo member who knows them personally has confirmed that they have that child; such a child has full access within the limits of the children's space. The transition between states occurs by itself, according to the position of the parent, and is not a measure.

A child under fifteen does not communicate or exchange with adults, not even with parental consent. An older child may do so with parental consent, and the parent then reads the conversation with the adult. The parent does not read conversations between children. A child does not enter the chain of confirmations, does not inscribe ZRNO, has no support programmes and does not participate in governance. A child's profile is not accessible to adults.

Anyone, including the child, can report that the link between a child and a parent is not true; the link is then suspended until the Foundation decides, and the person who submitted the report is not disclosed.

# 7. Contribution to the common good

## Founding contribution

The founding contribution is the work done before the platform opened: designing the system, building the protocol and the legal and organisational preparation. By nature it is the same as operational contribution, but it was done before any contribution channel was operating, so it could not be recorded while it was taking place. It cannot be recorded through operational contribution even afterwards: the daily limit of that channel is tied to the total number of recorded POEN, so while that number is zero the limit is zero too. The founders spent their own time and their own money on that work; the work is recorded, not the money spent.

The Protocol records that contribution afterwards, through a separate and transparent channel, up to an upper limit set in advance. It records it in steps of a fixed amount, tied to the growth of the total number of POEN, and not all at once, so that the entire amount would not move the coefficient at a single moment. The channel follows growth whatever it is: if the total number grows abruptly, the steps are also recorded abruptly, and the Rulebook on Founding Contribution does not promise that recording will be spread over a longer period. A step is recorded even when the growth comes from a single large contribution, so that the share of that contribution in the total number is thereby smaller; that effect is limited by the upper limit and ceases when the channel closes.

Since the step is fixed, the effect of one step on the coefficient decreases as the system grows. The aggregate founding contribution does not exceed approximately 24% of the total number of POEN, except when the total number decreases by annulment of records; in the first steps the share is smaller, and after the channel closes it decreases as the system grows. The figures are published in the Rulebook on Founding Contribution and in the Risk Acceptance Statement. That share carries no property right and is not a claim. The circle of founders is closed, the distribution among them is established by the Management Board by a decision on distribution, and the channel closes permanently upon reaching the upper limit. The result of the founding work enters the common good under open licences; the founders have no exclusive right to it.

## Donations

Every adult user can donate money to the Foundation. A donation is non-refundable: the donor acquires no right to a refund, no governance right in the Foundation and no share in the system, and obtains nothing by the donation. When the Foundation establishes receipt of a public donation, the Protocol records a contribution in the donor's record under the rules of the channel. Recording is an automatic act of the Protocol, independent of the act of donation, and is not consideration for it. The donation agreement therefore contains no number of POEN, coefficient or tier; it contains a statement that the contribution is recorded under the rules of the system, that the inscription is not consideration and that the donor has no claim to it.

The contribution is recorded with the recording coefficient, which depends on the sum of donations. A larger individual contribution has greater significance for the community: a larger contribution in one piece makes it possible to meet a specific need of the community (infrastructure, a programme or a project) at once. The recording coefficient is not a price of POEN and is not a discount on the donation, because nothing is acquired by a donation; it is a measure of recognition of the contribution. Nor is it the accounting coefficient.

The donor chooses whether the donation is public or anonymous. A public donation is listed in the list of donations with first name and surname, amount and date, without a pseudonym and without a link to a profile; the name can be withdrawn at any time. For an anonymous donation no POEN is recorded, not because of withheld consent, but because an inscription that cannot be attributed to any person would be an inscription that no one can verify.

Whoever obtains POEN from another user for money enters into a transaction that is prohibited, is concluded entirely outside the platform and has no protection whatsoever; they thereby acquire neither a donation tier, nor a donation confirmation, nor public recognition of contribution, and they expose themselves to the measures in the Terms of Use, up to exclusion. Cheating therefore does not pay: whoever buys POEN has bought a record without any protection, without a tier and without recognition, and the money went not to the community, but to one person.

## Sponsorship

A sponsor is a legal entity, an entrepreneur or the holder of a registered agricultural holding that supports the work of the Foundation by a donation of money or by an agreement on sponsorship in goods and services. A sponsor is not a user of the system and does not receive POEN or ZRNO. The contribution is recorded in the record of the user who submitted the application on behalf of the sponsor, under the same table as a personal donation; the sponsor's legal form does not affect the coefficient, and the sponsor's sum and the applicant's personal sum are kept separately. That is neither a gift from the sponsor to the user nor a distribution of profit.

Sponsorship in goods and services has a different mechanism. By an agreement with the Foundation the giver undertakes to hand over precisely specified goods without compensation, or to provide services, to participants who take vouchers. The Foundation does not receive or possess the goods; it is an agreement for the benefit of third parties, and the holder of a voucher has their own right against the giver. The contribution is therefore recorded at signing: what is recognised is the obligation that arose by signing and that holders of vouchers can enforce, not a future handover. The value of the contribution is the price from the giver's price list; the Foundation does not assess it. When a participant takes a voucher, a number of POEN derived from the inscription under the agreement is annulled in their record. If the giver does not perform the obligation, the agreement is terminated, the annulment is corrected for the holders, and what was recorded for the applicant for the unperformed vouchers is written off.

The sponsor register contains the name and, if submitted, the logo, and is displayed on the ranking list and on the vouchers. That is a record, not a right of the sponsor: the sponsor acquires no promotion, no advertising space and no advantage in the selection of offers in procurement.

## Operational contribution

Operational contribution is work outside the platform for the common good: organising events, technical work, help in the field. Tasks are posted, on behalf of the community, by the Foundation in the first phase, and in the second by Kolo keepers and the Upper Kolo. A participant signs up voluntarily and proposes the execution plan themselves. A task is divided into units, that is, parts of the work determined according to what is being carried out, and not according to time. The contributor states only how many units they have completed; they do not set the amount. Completion, and in a task with approval also the plan, are confirmed by Kolo keepers and a person authorised by the Management Board; the one who confirms cannot be a contributor to the same task.

The proposed POEN attached to a task is not a promised amount but a weight in the distribution of the common daily limit, which operational contribution shares with the support programmes. When the sum of the requested amounts is greater than the limit, all are reduced proportionally, and the excess is not carried over.

A special form of operational contribution is the cooperative contribution: organisational units in a cooperative, such as organising a distribution or running a joint action. The cooperative itself determines which units exist and to whom it assigns them, within the highest amount that its contributors can receive daily. That amount depends on the number of cooperative members, and a smaller cooperative receives proportionally more, because organisation is hardest for it. Completion is confirmed only by Kolo keepers, and not by the one who assigned the unit; the amount enters the common daily limit, and the unused part is not carried over. The cooperative bears other costs from its own record.

Operational contribution is not work for the Foundation. A commissioning party, a commissioned performance and compensation are lacking: the task is posted in connection with a need of the common good, not at the order of a person who would receive the performance for themselves; the contributor decides alone whether to sign up and how to work and may withdraw without consequences other than the absence of a record; the Foundation receives nothing upon completion of the task. POEN is not expressed as the value of working time.

## First listing

The first listing by which a user offers a good or a service, if it meets the content minimum and represents a genuine offer, carries a contribution once per person, also for a user who is not in the Kolo. The listing is published immediately, and the contribution is recorded when the Foundation approves it. The channel exists because the first listing is the user's first step on the platform: by it they show good intention to open their personal capacities (goods, services or knowledge) to the community.

## Farms

A constant supply of food and agricultural products fills the exchange space: that is what is sought in every place every day. That is why a farm has its own channel. A farm is applied for by an adult user; entry in the register is not a condition, and a small household that sells eggs participates just like a registered farm. An extract from the register is not submitted, and before approval the Foundation talks with the holder, for the purpose of approving the farm, not of confirming reality.

A farm is not a user and has no record in the Protocol. The contribution is noted for the farm members in the order of joining, for a limited number of them, and recorded when the member has posted an offer and when they are in the Kolo. All members who are Kolo members manage the farm equally, and one person is responsible for the declaration of accuracy of data. When the farm terminates, on any ground, the recognition of the contribution tied to it also ceases; that is not a measure. Exclusion is imposed only on the one who gave a false declaration or knew of it.

A farm and a cooperative are not the same thing: a farm is a family that produces, and a cooperative is the community of a place. A cooperative is not needed for a farm to participate, and a farm member may also be a cooperative member.

Listings on behalf of an approved farm carry the DOMAĆE mark, which is derived, not assigned: it indicates only that the listing was posted on behalf of a farm that has joined the system, and the Foundation does not guarantee the origin, quality or safety of the good.

## Cooperatives

A cooperative in the system is a cooperative registered under the Law on Cooperatives, with its own organs and obligations; the Foundation has neither a vote nor a consent in its organs. A cooperative is the place where the community of one settlement organises itself: it coordinates exchange, distribution, joint tasks and the use of shared equipment, and helps disputes among the users of its place to be resolved amicably. A cooperative is also a place where help is given to those who do not cope with the platform, especially the elderly: it helps them use it, while the account remains theirs and no one acts in their place. It itself determines how many POEN it records for what it receives, issues and provides for use; the Foundation does not determine that. The Protocol recognises the growth of a cooperative as a contribution to the common good: when the number of cooperative members reaches a threshold from a table set in advance, POEN is recorded in the cooperative's record, once per threshold. A cooperative has its own record in the Protocol; POEN is re-registered from and to it only on a ground under the Rulebook on Cooperatives, and the amounts are set by the cooperative, never by the Foundation. The first cooperative in a place is entered only when there are enough participants there, with the support of the Foundation; for each subsequent one that condition does not apply, and the record of every cooperative is opened when it has enough cooperative members.

A cooperative is not a supplier in a collective procurement. What a cooperative can provide is provided within the system, through exchange and distribution, and procurement serves only goods that cannot be provided within the system; the community's dinar funds do not compete with exchange.

## Support programmes

Some forms of participation in the community are continuous and are not visible through individual exchanges: parenthood, old age, life with reduced capacity or after the loss of a home, studying. The support programmes (Mothers Support, Elderly Support, Special Support and Student Support) recognise that participation by recording a contribution every day. The Mothers Support programme bears that name because childcare most often falls on mothers and remains invisible in every record of work; every primary carer of a child has the same right, regardless of sex. New records increase the total number of recorded POEN and thereby move the coefficient for everyone; that is a deliberate design decision. Verifiability remains complete: the sum of the daily amounts under the programmes, together with the other channels, gives the change in the total number of POEN. Similar recognition of care for the elderly exists in the Japanese Fureai Kippu system and in the Swiss Zeitvorsorge (St. Gallen); there hours are recorded, with a promise of future service, while in the KOLO system nothing is promised, so the record is not a claim.

A programme requires a participant and express consent before any step. Documents are not submitted. That the conditions are met is confirmed by a Kolo member who knows the applicant personally, and if there is no such person, by the Foundation; the application is approved by the Foundation. Recording is not compensation: nothing is paid by it and nothing is required in return. By the inscription a support recipient receives neither money nor a thing, and the record cannot be turned into money or collected, nor assigned as a claim (chapter 11). The programmes are carried out in pursuit of the Foundation's objectives under the Statute, in particular social protection and solidarity support; they do not replace rights from the social protection system and are not conditional on waiving them. Participants see the recording with the pseudonym, the name of the programme and the amount; the ground on which the right was exercised is not shown to anyone.

## Children's space

In the children's space new records arise in one way: through a friendship of two Kolo children made in person. The contribution is tied to that friendship. When the friendship is ended, what was recorded for both sides on its basis is written off, even when the record thereby goes into the negative. The reason is the same as elsewhere when the record does not stop at zero: a child who has already re-registered the POEN from the friendship must not come off differently from a child who kept it. Upon coming of age the contribution from friendships is annulled: it belongs to the children's space and does not pass into the adult's ledger, and this channel, which is not under the daily limit, is thereby also limited by the time the child spends in that space. Children are informed of this in advance, and the child is notified of every write-off.

Children do not participate in the governance of the system, but they do participate in shaping their own space. In the children's space there is the Wish Box: a child can propose a new function, game or rule for the children's space and support other children's proposals, one vote per proposal. Proposals are displayed only in the children's space, without names and pseudonyms, and before display they are reviewed by the Foundation, so that they contain no personal data or inappropriate content. To the proposals that receive the most support the Foundation responds with reasons, in language the child understands: what it will do, what it will not do and why. A parent sees the text of their child's proposals. A proposal does not bring POEN and does not bind the Foundation, and children thus get a way to be heard about the space that was made for them.

# 8. Projects and collective procurement

When the Foundation's dinar funds exceed the operating costs, they can be allocated to projects for the benefit of the community. The basic project is collective procurement: the Foundation procures from a third party a good that users cannot provide within the system, and participants collect it in equal shares, with annulment of records upon use. Another project is the distribution of vouchers from sponsorship in goods and services, in which the Foundation procures and pays for nothing.

Collective procurement is not an economic activity. The Foundation earns no revenue or profit from it, does not offer goods on the market and must not receive compensation for a good provided, either from users or from a third party. Shares that are not collected are not sold. Towards the supplier the Foundation is the buyer; the user does not enter into a contractual relationship with the supplier.

The number of POEN per share is not the price of the good. It is set before offers are collected, according to the community's need, and is not derived from the dinar price, which is not known at that time. That is why the dinar side of the procurement (all offers, the selected supplier and the purchase price) is published in full, but separately, in the report on the use of funds: in the same place as the number of POEN per share it would allow a ratio that does not exist to be read by division.

Only participants take part in procurement: without that condition a place in the order would be attainable by re-registering POEN to a new account. A good that someone already offers on the Pijaca is not procured: exchange is protected from competition with the community's funds, and the need is referred to the existing listing. Shares are set so that they are small and that there are enough of them for all who have signed up; the order according to recorded contribution decides only when there are not enough shares. The origin of POEN is not examined, and all channels count equally.

The project and its parameters are decided by the Management Board in the first phase, and by the Upper Kolo in the second; since the decision concerns dinar funds, the decision of the Upper Kolo is a recommendation to the Management Board.

# 9. Governance

## Progressive decentralisation

Every system has rules, and the question is who sets them, how and under what constraints. KOLO answers this with progressive decentralisation, a path from centralised to decentralised governance, with a measurable condition of transition (cf. Walden, 2020). In common practice the founding team itself judges when it is time to hand over governance; in the KOLO system the condition is written in advance and the transition occurs by itself, without anyone's decision. Decentralised governance requires what does not exist at the beginning: enough people for decisions to be representative, experience with the rules and a stable basis. That is why governance begins with the Foundation, and passes to the community when those conditions are met.

## The first phase

In the first phase the rules of the system are established by the Management Board of the Foundation by acts of the Foundation; an amendment may also be proposed by the founder of the Foundation. Even in that phase no amendment can change the four principles or the limits of the common good, or enable its appropriation.

## The second phase and the Upper Kolo

When the total number of recorded POEN reaches the threshold set by the Rulebook on the KOLO System, the system automatically moves into the second phase: the inscription of ZRNO opens and the Upper Kolo is established. One threshold, one transition, which cannot be reversed even when the total number later falls; the Management Board only notes it by an act that is declaratory. That sum includes contribution from all channels, including donations; the transition therefore does not depend on the will of the Management Board, and occurs when enough contribution has been recognised in the system.

The Upper Kolo consists of Kolo keepers who have activated ZRNO, including the founders. The composition is not established by appointment: the Protocol establishes it from the ledger for each accounting period separately, together with the voting power of each member. The Upper Kolo establishes proposals on the rules of the system, on the introduction and abolition of modules and on other questions that affect the common good. A proposal may be submitted by any participant; it is voted on when at least one Kolo keeper supports it, and the Foundation does not decide whether a proposal will be voted on. Every proposal goes the same three-day route: on the first day it is submitted, on the second Kolo keepers support it, on the third it is voted on.

The Upper Kolo is a body of the Foundation established by the Rulebook on the KOLO System, on the basis of the authority of the Management Board under the Statute, and not an organ of the Foundation. It does not represent the Foundation, does not dispose of its assets, and its decision does not by itself create rights or obligations towards third parties. The Management Board adopts a decision of the Upper Kolo by its own act. It undertook to do so by the same act by which the Upper Kolo was established: to adopt the act without delay and without assessing expediency, and to reject it only on a closed list of grounds: if the decision is contrary to the law or the Statute, or if it encroaches on a question excluded from the competence of the Upper Kolo. A rejection is reasoned and published, and in the act by which it adopts or rejects a decision the Management Board states how its members voted on it in the Upper Kolo. On dinar funds the Upper Kolo gives recommendations, and the Management Board gives a reasoned answer to each. Competence thus remains where the law places it, and the Foundation binds itself by its own act to act on the decision.

## Square-root voting and delegation

The Upper Kolo decides by square-root voting: the voting power of a Kolo keeper is equal to the integer value of the square root of the number of their active ZRNO. ZRNO is not a share in anything (neither in the assets of the Foundation, nor in the system, nor in any material good), but a record of position from which a vote arises; it carries no right to a part of anything, so voting power is not proportional to any holding either. The idea that voting power grows more slowly than the quantity from which it arises is known from Penrose's square-root rule (Penrose, 1946) and from quadratic voting (Posner and Weyl, 2018). KOLO applies neither of these two mechanisms, but a constant voting weight by the square root of active ZRNO; the similarity is independent. The root slows concentration, but does not prevent it: that protection holds as long as each vote belongs to a different real person: whoever split the same contribution across several accounts would have greater aggregate power, and ZRNO held by several close people, or power gathered by delegation with one keeper, can give more than the root would give from a single record. That is why the system does not rely on the formula alone: how concentrated voting power is, is reported for each accounting period (chapter 14). That is why proof of reality matters for governance too, not only for exchange. Voting power arises exclusively from the activated ZRNO of a confirmed Kolo keeper, and not directly from the number of POEN or from dinar donations; POEN, including POEN recorded on the basis of a donation, leads to a vote only through the inscription and activation of ZRNO (chapter 10). ZRNO is not consumed by voting.

A Kolo keeper may delegate their voting power to another Kolo keeper (cf. Ford, 2002; Blum and Zuber, 2016). Voting power is delegated, not ZRNO; delegation is general and can be revoked at any time, and delegated power is added to the delegate's own power. In this way the vote of those who cannot take part in every decision is not lost. A delegate may also delegate in turn, so power passes along the chain to the one who votes in person; a chain that closes into a loop counts for no one. The power of each keeper is rooted separately, so a delegate is credited with powers that have already been rooted. Experience with delegation shows that power can gather with a small number of delegates (Kling et al., 2015). That is why composition and power are established for each day separately, and any delegation can be revoked at any time, with effect from the following day. Delegation is also a path of concentration: a delegate votes with the power of all who have entrusted it to them. That is why it is also reported how much power is delegated and how many delegates together hold a majority.

## Limits of decision-making

The Upper Kolo does not have unlimited power. No decision can abolish the four principles, because the system would thereby change its legal nature, nor the limits of the common good: data minimisation and open licences. A decision that requires spending the Foundation's dinar funds has the effect of a recommendation, and the statutory obligations of the Management Board remain in every case. After the transition to the second phase the Foundation retains no brake other than those set by the law and the Statute.

## What governance is not

Governance of the KOLO system is not governance of a company: there are no shareholders or dividends. Nor is it governance of a state: participation is voluntary, and whoever does not agree with decisions retains the right to exit (cf. Hirschman, 1970). It is governance of a common good: Kolo keepers with recorded contribution and position decide on the rules of a system that belongs to everyone, with a right of use, contribution and participation in governance, but not of disposal.

# 10. Why cooperation makes sense: incentives and safeguards

The preceding chapters describe what the system is. This chapter explains why it can be expected to work, drawing on the theory of mechanism design (Hurwicz, 1973), the logic of collective action (Olson, 1965), the governance of common goods (Ostrom, 1990) and the evolution of cooperation in repeated relationships (Axelrod, 1984). That is not a promise: the system does not guarantee that everyone will benefit, nor that abuse will not be attempted. What is described is what has been built into the design, and where the design leaves questions open.

## The user

A user benefits directly from exchange: they get what they need from someone who has it, and give what they have to someone who needs it. The more people there are in the system, the more likely it is that they will find both; this eases the problem of the double coincidence of wants that limits barter (Jevons, 1875).

The system must also solve the problem of the first step: it has value only when there are enough people, and people have no reason to come while there are not. KOLO solves it through existing social networks: the first users come through the chain of confirmations, from people who know them personally, so the early community has both trust and real possibilities of exchange. The second support is everyday supply: food from farms and what the cooperative organises in the place is what is sought every day, so exchange has something to gather around even before there are many people in the system. Contribution recorded from the beginning remains in the ledger; that is a position in the ledger, not the value of a record. The system promises no return whatsoever, and the accounting coefficient can also fall.

## Introducing others without a pyramid

Bringing in new users through the chain of confirmations has none of the elements of a scheme in which benefit is acquired by introducing others. Nothing is paid for entering the system or for any status. Nothing is paid out of the system: POEN cannot be converted into money. The one who confirms or introduces a new participant and that participant record the same amount, set in advance, once per person, and only when the new participant has made a first contribution themselves. The amount does not grow with the number of people introduced, is not passed upwards through the chain, and the one who brought the introducer into the Kolo records nothing on that ground. A user's benefit comes from exchange and their own contribution, not from the number of people they have introduced.

## The one who confirms

The one who confirms is motivated in two ways: confirmation is a contribution to the integrity of the system, and as the network expands their choice in exchange grows too. For every confirmation and every introduction they answer with their own position (chapter 6): a false one brings little, and only after a human decision on the first contribution, while the loss is greater than the gain. The rational choice is to confirm only someone you really know.

## The Kolo keeper

A Kolo keeper has the incentives of every user and participation in governance. Since they themselves exchange in the system, they have an interest in the system being well run.

A Kolo keeper can also write off free ZRNO, with POEN recorded at the accounting coefficient applicable at that time. That possibility is not an incentive: the difference compared with the number of POEN used at inscription can arise in either direction, no one promises it, and one's own inscription and write-off do not move the coefficient. The difference exists only in POEN, which cannot leave the system. Whoever wants a vote must activate ZRNO and thereby gives up write-off, so they cannot have both at the same time.

## Exchange and the ledger

Every system that records contribution with an internal unit faces the question of whether the rules encourage circulation or accumulation of records (Gesell, 1916; Lietaer, 2001). In the KOLO system exchange moves existing records: whoever gives a good gets a record; whoever receives a good reduces their record. New records arise only through the contribution channels that the acts recognise.

The system deliberately does not reward exchange itself. If every exchange brought a new record, two users could exchange back and forth without any real good, solely for the sake of the inscription. Since exchange does not increase the total number of POEN, a fake exchange brings no one anything: what one gets, the other loses. The benefit of exchange is not in the record, but in the good or service that someone received; the record only notes that the exchange took place.

Whether such a design yields enough exchange is a question of experience. The answer is given by measuring the live state of the system (chapter 14), not by assumption.

## Donation and recorded contribution

The Foundation spends donations on the objectives for which it was founded. When a user donates money, the Protocol, under the rules of the system and independently of the donation agreement, records a contribution in POEN for them. The number depends on the amount and on the tier determined by the sum of their donations, because a larger contribution has greater significance for the community; the range of the recording coefficient is set by the Rulebook on Sponsorship and Donations. That POEN has the same status as any other: it is used in exchange within the system, it is the measure of priority in collective procurement and it counts towards the inscription of ZRNO, from whose activation a Kolo keeper acquires a vote in the Upper Kolo. That is why a user who donates can reach those possibilities earlier than a user who only exchanges.

A donation gives none of those possibilities directly. It is non-refundable and without consideration: by it the donor acquires no good, service or claim against the Foundation or any other person; the donation agreement contains no number of POEN, tier or recording coefficient, and the donor has no right to the inscription. Recorded contribution does not form part of anyone's assets, cannot be taken out of the system, exchanged for money or collected, and the Foundation pays nothing out of the ledger. ZRNO is not transferred and has no price. The accounting coefficient grows when more POEN are recorded in the system, and falls when POEN are annulled; no one sets or promises it, and on the write-off of ZRNO one may also get fewer POEN than were used for the inscription. Only a confirmed Kolo keeper with activated ZRNO has a vote; voting power grows with the square root of activated ZRNO, and inscription is limited per day. The inscription of ZRNO can also be reached without money: by confirming and introducing others into the Kolo, by a first listing, by farm contribution, by operational contribution and by exchange, as a rule more slowly.

## Abuse costs more than cooperation

Ostrom counts monitoring and graduated sanctions among the key protections of a common good. KOLO has several features that make abuse costlier than cooperation.

A false account requires someone who is really in the network to put their own position at stake. The cost of an attack is not a forged document, but the corruption of a real person in the network of trust. That protection is strongest where the network of acquaintances is dense; as the system grows, especially outside the environment in which people are connected, the risk of coordinated false confirmations grows, and with it the weight of supervision (chapter 15).

ZRNO cannot be transferred or sold, so governance power cannot be gathered by buying it up or turned into money. POEN cannot be taken out of the system, so a manipulated ledger has no external value; whoever undermines the integrity of the system also endangers the system in which they themselves participate. That does not mean that the goods and services being exchanged have no value outside the system as well (a kilogram of honey has its price at the market), but that there is no route by which a record from the ledger could be exchanged for dinars.

The rules are public, decisions reasoned, and the ledger is available to participants in pseudonymous form. Supervision of confirmations and introductions, exchange reports and listing reports are active safeguards; their rules are established by the acts.

## Equilibrium

The aim of the design is for legitimate participation to be a more favourable choice than abuse or non-participation, so that no user has a reason to change behaviour unilaterally (Nash, 1950). That is a design intention, not a proven claim: it is tested only by experience, through aggregate data on exchange, abuse and the operation of protective measures. The system is not immune to abuse, but a system in which abuse is costly, visible and of limited reach has better prospects than a system that relies only on good will (Olson, 1965; Ostrom, 1990).

# 11. What KOLO is not

## The pattern

This chapter is the only place in which the Whitepaper speaks about legal classification. The binding provisions are in the Rulebook on the KOLO System and in the separate rulebooks. The pattern is the same everywhere: it is not only the label that is asserted (“POEN is not money”), but the elements of the legal concept that are missing are enumerated. A label can be disputed; the absence of an element is a fact that can be checked in the system.

## Four principles

The legal position of the system rests on four principles built into the design:

- non-convertibility: no accounting unit can be converted into money, currency or any means with value outside the system, directly or indirectly, including exchange for vouchers, gift cards or another means that can be turned into money, transferred to a third person or used with a merchant of one's choice. The distribution of vouchers from sponsorship in goods and services is not that. The voucher is provided by the giver under an agreement for the benefit of third parties, and the giver does not receive POEN. The Foundation possesses and pays for nothing in the distribution. The voucher is issued in the name of the holder and for a specified good with one giver, and is not turned into money;
- absence of property rights over records: POEN and ZRNO records are data in the ledger of the common good, not assets of the user; they are not transferred or inherited as property rights and are not a claim against the Foundation;
- non-refundability of donations: donation and sponsorship are non-refundable; the Foundation spends the money received on the objectives in the Statute and does not keep it as backing for anyone's record;
- automatic recording: the recording of POEN is an automatic act of the Protocol under the rules of the system, and not a counter-performance for a donation; there is no agreement on exchanging dinars for POEN.

The four principles are not rules that the system can change by itself, but the limits within which it exists. They cannot be abolished by any decision, not even by a decision of the Upper Kolo.

Among them non-convertibility has a central role: because a record cannot leave the system, a difference in position cannot become a return, and a record cannot become a means of payment. The other three principles do not depend on it, but without it they would not be sufficient.

Alongside the principles stand two limits of the common good, equally unchangeable, but for a different reason. Data minimisation protects people: only the data necessary for the functioning of the system are collected. Open licences protect the common good from being closed. The principles determine the legal nature of the system, and the limits what the common good is.

## POEN is not money, a currency or a digital asset

Users re-register POEN to one another in connection with the exchange of goods and services; that is a fact. POEN lacks the other elements of a digital asset:

- the Foundation does not sell it or issue it for money;
- sale between users is prohibited by the Terms of Use, and if it happens, it happens outside the platform, without any protection and without effect in the system;
- it is not exchanged for money or for other assets;
- it has no price and no market;
- it does not exist outside the Protocol's ledger.

It cannot be obtained by purchase: the Foundation does not sell it and does not issue it against compensation, and the inscription is not consideration for any giving. For a donation the contribution is recorded under the rules of the system, independently of the donation agreement; that link is described openly in chapter 10. It cannot be alienated against compensation: dealing in POEN for value outside the system is prohibited by the Terms of Use. It is not redeemed and is not converted, so there is no exit from it into money. The Foundation does not set a price for it, does not offer it on the market, does not publish any ratio of POEN to the dinar and does not apply any such ratio. It does not exist outside the Protocol's ledger and cannot be transferred to a record that the Protocol does not keep. It does not serve to settle monetary obligations and cannot be offered instead of money.

With electronic money and digital units that are bought, the buyer's money goes to the issuer and remains behind the unit: as a reserve from which the unit is redeemed, or as a fund on which its price depends. In the KOLO system there is none of that. Money that the Foundation receives by donation is not kept as backing for anyone's record and is not owed to anyone who has POEN. It is spent for its purpose, on the objectives of the Foundation under the Statute (procurements, projects, infrastructure), and no part of it is kept as backing for POEN. When it is spent, no record in the ledger changes. The sum of all POEN and the balance of the Foundation's account are two independent things.

## POEN is not a payment service or electronic money

A POEN record is not a claim either against the Foundation or against any other person. It is not issued in exchange for a payment: the contribution on the basis of a donation is recorded under the rules of the system, independently of the act of donation. In no ledger update does the Foundation receive, hold or transfer users' monetary funds. Re-registration is not charged for. There is no payout, redemption or conversion from a record. A person who has no record in the ledger does not receive POEN, and neither does the giver of a voucher receive POEN: the giver performs its own agreement with the Foundation for the benefit of third parties.

## POEN is not income

By the inscription of POEN a user receives neither money nor a thing. Nothing passes from the assets of the Foundation into the assets of the user, and the record that arises cannot be turned into money, assigned as a claim or collected. Re-registering a record to another user in connection with an exchange is not an assignment: the record is not a claim, so there is nothing to assign; rather, the Protocol updates the ledger. The Foundation makes no payout on the basis of the inscription on any ground, does not establish a value of POEN expressed in money and applies no such ratio. In the system the dinar touches only the Foundation's account.

## ZRNO is not an investment

The position of a Kolo keeper lacks the elements that make an investment an investment. ZRNO is not obtained by investing funds, but is inscribed from contribution already recorded in the record, including contribution recorded on the basis of a donation, as described in chapter 10; that contribution too leads to ZRNO only through inscription, like any other. The Foundation does not sell it, does not offer it and does not set a price for it. There is no return that could be realised: a change of position is expressed only in POEN, which cannot leave the system. There is no effort by another person directed at creating a benefit for the Kolo keeper: the accounting coefficient is the quotient of two sums from the ledger that the Protocol calculates without discretion, no person manages or promises it, and the Kolo keeper does not move it by their own inscription or write-off. Nor is the Kolo keeper separated from governance: their vote arises from activated ZRNO. For a member without confirmation for whom ZRNO has been inscribed the first three reasons apply; they acquire a vote not by a stake, but by confirmation, which is not obtained with funds.

Nor is ZRNO a digital token: it does not represent any property right in digital form: neither a right to money, nor to a thing, nor to a part of revenue or assets.

## A donation buys nothing

A donation acquires no good, no claim against the Foundation, no right to a refund and no right to redemption of POEN, nor directly any influence in decision-making or a place in the order in procurement; the recording of contribution is not a counter-performance and is not determined by agreement. The indirect link between donation, recorded contribution, ZRNO and priority is described openly in chapter 10.

Nor is a donation a deposit: it is not returned, bears no interest and the Foundation does not hold it for the donor, but spends it on the objectives in the Statute. Funds that the Foundation receives from public sources and in calls for proposals are spent for the purpose for which they were given and are not recorded in anyone's POEN record.

## The Foundation does not act on the market

The Foundation does not offer users goods or services against compensation, does not charge for the use of the platform, does not mediate in exchange and receives nothing on the basis of exchange. It is not a party to the exchange between users. When a user offers a good or a service within their business activity, the regulations that apply to it (on registration, trade, food safety, taxes and consumer protection) apply even when the exchange has been agreed through the platform, and recording in POEN does not change the classification of the transaction.

## Operational contribution is not work for the Foundation

Operational contribution does not establish an employment relationship or work outside an employment relationship, in particular not a contract for services, because a commissioning party, a commissioned performance and compensation are lacking (chapter 7). The same applies to founding work and to children's exchange, which is not work that a child performs for another.

## The Upper Kolo is not an organ

The Upper Kolo is a body established by a rulebook, and not an organ of the Foundation; the organs of the Foundation are the Management Board and the Director. A decision of the Upper Kolo is not a general act of the Foundation: it is implemented by the Management Board by its own act, in the manner set out in the Rulebook on the KOLO System (chapter 9).

## Collective procurement is not an economic activity

The Foundation earns no revenue or profit from procurement, does not offer goods on the market and receives no compensation for a good provided. The annulment of a record is not compensation for a good (chapter 8).

## KOLO is not a crypto project

There is no token that is issued, traded or listed on an exchange, there is no public offering and there is no blockchain. KOLO uses a centralised ledger on infrastructure provided by the Foundation. The absence of a blockchain is not in itself an argument for classification, because regulations on digital assets are technologically neutral; the classification is determined by the elements in the preceding sections. Decentralisation in the KOLO system is not technical but a matter of governance.

## Where KOLO is located

KOLO is not a private company, is not a state institution and is not a cooperative. The Foundation is indeed non-profit, but the system is broader than it. Serbian law has no ready-made category for a system like this, but it has enough institutes to describe it: the foundation gives legal personality, the licences protect the common good, and the contractual relationship governed by the Terms of Use governs the relationship with the user. KOLO does not seek new law; the system fits functionally into the social and solidarity economy that international institutions recognise (Appendix A).

The legal position is not a defence against regulation, but a design that from the outset takes into account where the system sits within the legal order.

## Risks

The risks of participation, from a fall in the accounting coefficient and the non-refundability of donations to technical, tax and regulatory risks, are described in the Risk Acceptance Statement, the only act of the KOLO system that describes them.

# 12. Personal data

By its nature the system processes personal data: the chain of confirmations, the contribution ledger, data on donations, and in the support programmes and the children's space also special categories of data and children's data. Protection is built into the design, in accordance with the Law on Personal Data Protection; the controller is the Foundation.

Records in the ledger are tied to pseudonyms, not to names. Pseudonymity is not anonymity: pseudonymised data remain personal data. The link between a pseudonym and a first name and surname exists, but only in the identity register, which is kept separately from the contribution ledger, is filled in only in the cases established by the Rulebook on the KOLO System and is not shown to other users except in the cases that the acts expressly provide for. The unique master citizen number and data from identity documents are not entered in the identity register, and copies of documents are not kept. When a cooperative helps in a dispute, the data are given to it by the parties themselves; the Foundation does not supply data about users to the cooperative.

Only necessary data are collected. What a user enters voluntarily is not a condition either for proof of reality or for access to functions. Neither the IP address nor a device fingerprint is recorded with the record of consent. Special categories of data are processed only with express consent, which can be withdrawn.

Data are stored on infrastructure provided by the Foundation, with processors with whom a data processing agreement has been concluded. The application runs and the database is located in the European Union; when individual processors process data outside it, this is done with contractual safeguards described in the Privacy Policy.

The right to erasure and the integrity of the ledger are reconciled by separating identifying and accounting data. When a user leaves the system, the email address, voluntary data and the name in the identity register are deleted, while the numerical history remains under a pseudonym that does not point to the name, because deleting the records of one user would damage the ledger on which others rely. That is pseudonymisation, not anonymisation; the retained records are still personal data and enjoy full protection. The amount, the parties, the time and the type of record do not change, but the description of a record that contains personal data is deleted when the basis for its processing ceases, by withdrawal of consent or by termination of status. After the expiry of the period set by the Rulebook on the KOLO System the pseudonym attached to the records is replaced by a label that does not point to the person even indirectly, so from then on the records are no longer personal data.

The details (purposes, legal bases, time limits, recipients and the rights of data subjects) are established by the Privacy Policy, the Records of Processing Activities and the Data Protection Impact Assessment.

# 13. Expansion into other environments

Bačka has a long cooperative tradition: in Bački Petrovac in 1846 one of the first cooperative societies in this part of Europe was founded.

KOLO is not replicated, but expands. No new systems, new foundations or new ledgers arise: every new environment enters the same system, with one Protocol, one ledger of the common good and one Foundation.

The system is carried into a new environment from within, through membership. People from the new place enter the Kolo through the same chain of confirmations, through someone who knows them personally or through the call list, and build their own network of acquaintances, their own Pijaca and their own farms. When there are enough of them, they found a cooperative in their place, which becomes part of the system and through which the local community organises joint production, equipment and distribution, sets its own rules of exchange and helps disputes in the place to be resolved amicably. Each place thus gets its own face, and the system remains one. Connecting places (exchange and transport of goods between cooperatives, as well as support for founding in a new place) is governed by a separate module of inter-cooperative logistics.

What is carried over are the solutions to the three problems from chapter 1: trust through a chain of people who know each other, scaling through the contribution ledger, and a legal form that takes regulatory classification into account from the outset. What is not carried over is the network of acquaintances, which every environment builds anew. Expansion beyond Serbia is a module decided on by the Upper Kolo, with a legal analysis of the target jurisdiction: on data protection, digital assets, payment services, foundations, cooperatives and taxes.

Open licences allow someone else to take over the code and the rules, but that is not the way in which KOLO expands: such a system is not KOLO, does not bear its name, and its records are not connected in any form with the ledger of the KOLO Protocol.

# 14. What is measured as success

Success is measured only over what is alive. The total number of accounts ever opened or of records ever inscribed is not the state of the system; closed accounts and the return of records to the Protocol when an account is closed are neither community nor exchange.

The state of the system consists of active accounts, active listings of active accounts and exchanges (re-registrations that accompany a real exchange between active accounts). The most important measure of critical mass is exchange among participants who are not founders: only when people exchange with one another, and not only with those who launched the system, does the community exist. Children are counted separately, never among users.

Health of the common good. Alongside the state of the system, what shows whether the common good is alive is reported: how many people exchange and how many exchanges take place between people who are not founders; how many people come into the Kolo through personal acquaintance, and how many through the call list, and how long they wait for a call; how many farms constantly offer food; in how many places there is a cooperative and how many cooperative members there are; how the Foundation spends its funds and how much has gone into projects. Dinar indicators are reported separately from POEN. Every measure is reported as the state on a given day, not as a sum from the beginning.

Concentration. It is also reported how far the system is concentrated in few hands: how many recorded POEN are held by the largest records and by the founders' records; what share the founding contribution has in the total number of POEN; how much ZRNO is inscribed and how much of it is held by those who have the most; how many delegates together hold a majority of voting power and how much power is delegated. The report produces no effect whatsoever (it does not restrict inscription, voting or delegation), but lets the community see what it is deciding about.

All indicators are aggregate and relate to the state, not to flows: the system does not track who re-registered how much to whom. A sum that covers fewer than five people is not reported, so that an individual could not be recognised from it. Children are counted separately and are not included in the concentration indicators.

Whether exchange through the system keeps more value in the place (what the literature measures with the local multiplier; Sacks, 2002), the system does not claim in advance; that can be checked only when there is enough exchange.

Success is not measured by the accounting coefficient. The coefficient is not an indicator of success: it grows when contribution is inscribed and falls when records are annulled, and says nothing about whether people are exchanging.

# 15. Development trajectory, open questions and limits

## Trajectory

KOLO is not a finished product, but a system that is built gradually, tested in practice and changed on the basis of experience. The trajectory has no dates; it has conditions. The KOLO Foundation was entered in the register in 2026 in Sombor, the platform operates at ekolo.rs, and the system is in the first phase: the rules are established by the Management Board, the founders are the only Kolo keepers, and the experience of the first users shows which rules seem logical on paper but not in practice. The second phase begins when the threshold of the total number of recorded POEN is reached: then the inscription of ZRNO opens, the Upper Kolo arises and governance passes to the community. After that the system develops under its own rules (new rules, new programmes, new environments), under the governance of the community. The transition to the second phase is the end of the founding period, not the end of development. Two modules lie ahead of the system: inter-cooperative logistics, which connects cooperatives of different places, and expansion beyond Serbia.

## Open questions

The system lists the questions to which it does not yet have a definitive answer, because honesty towards users and regulators is more important than the appearance of completeness.

Inheritance. POEN and ZRNO are not property rights and are not inherited as property. Upon a user's death their status terminates, ZRNO returns to the Protocol without POEN being recorded, and the numerical history remains under a pseudonym. That position could be challenged, because the record is used within the system; a definitive answer may depend on the development of case law on digital records.

Scaling proof of reality. One confirmation per person makes it easier for the network to expand, but the one who confirms thereby becomes the only support for those they have brought into the Kolo. The call list opens a route for those who know no one, but a conversation is a weaker support than personal acquaintance; that is why whoever is introduced after a conversation remains a member without confirmation until someone who knows them confirms them. How to preserve the integrity of the graph with hundreds of thousands of people, especially outside an environment with a dense network of acquaintances, remains open (cf. Douceur, 2002).

The tax system. The exchange of goods and services between users may have tax consequences under the regulations that apply to exchange in general, especially when it is carried out by a person within their business activity; the fact that the exchange is recorded in POEN does not change that classification. The inscription of POEN itself is not income, because it lacks the elements of income (chapter 11). The competent authorities may take a different view on particular phenomena; the risks are described in the Risk Acceptance Statement.

Dependence on the initial core. Complementary systems have often weakened when the circle of people who launched them withdrew. That is why the most important measure is exchange among those who are not founders (chapter 14).

No credit. Mutual credit networks let a member take before they give, and in a crisis this can soften a fall in exchange (Stodder, 2009, on WIR). KOLO does not give credit: a new user starts from zero, and the first records arise from their contribution. Whether this slows the start of exchange, experience will show.

Limits of growth. The total number of ZRNO is unchangeable, and the coefficient has no upper limit. Whether some quantity will become impractical with a large number of people, experience will show.

Concentration. The total number of ZRNO is unchangeable, and the root and the limit on inscription per accounting period slow accumulation, but do not prevent it. Earlier and larger contribution, including a large donation, may mean that few people hold a large part of the inscribed ZRNO; sharing ZRNO among close people and delegation can circumvent the root. The system does not respond to this with new prohibitions, but by reporting concentration (chapter 14) and by the rules being decided by the Upper Kolo, whose decisions and vote totals are available to users. A decision on the rules that does not spend dinars, but indirectly affects the Foundation's finances, binds the Management Board; that is the cost of transferring governance. Whether that is enough, experience will show.

## Limits that are not crossed

The four principles and the two limits of the common good are maintained by the system along the whole trajectory. Abolishing the principles would change the legal nature of the system: from a participatory common-good system into a financial instrument, a payment service or an investment scheme. Abolishing the limits would turn it into an instrument of surveillance or into a closed system. That is why they are set as elements of the architecture, and not as parameters that governance changes. Everything else (amounts, time limits, thresholds) are operational parameters that can and should change with experience, through the procedures in the acts.

# 16. Conclusion

KOLO answers the three problems of shared exchange (scaling, trust and the legal framework) in one system; whether the answer works as intended, experience shows. The answer is a contribution ledger kept by the Protocol, in two separate units; proof of reality that rests on people who know each other, not on documents; a Foundation that gives the common good a legal form and is not its owner; and governance that passes to the community at a threshold known in advance.

The four principles (non-convertibility, absence of property rights over records, non-refundability of donations and automatic recording) ensure that the system cannot become a financial instrument, a payment service or an investment scheme, and are the basis of its legal classification. Data minimisation and open licences, as limits of the common good, ensure that it does not become an instrument of surveillance or a closed system.

The system knows its limitations: whether there will be enough exchange, how proof of reality behaves when the network grows and how the competent authorities will classify particular phenomena. These questions are stated openly, because that is part of the design, not a shortcoming of the documentation.

# Appendix B: Ostrom's Eight Principles and KOLO

From research on communities that have governed common goods over a long period, Elinor Ostrom derived eight principles (Ostrom, 1990). They were formulated for rival goods: pastures, fishponds, water. KOLO is a predominantly non-rival digital common good, with two rival elements: the total number of ZRNO is unchangeable, and exchange moves existing records (Hess and Ostrom, 2007). That is why some principles take a different form in the KOLO system.

1. Clear boundaries. Who is in the Kolo is established by the chain of confirmations; rights by status are established by the acts; the common good is protected by the four principles and the two limits of the common good (data minimisation and open licences). The boundaries are built into the software, and not only into custom.

2. Rules adapted to the place. The rules of the system are set by people, not by an algorithm: in the first phase the Management Board, in the second the Upper Kolo. In its own place a cooperative itself, through its organs, determines how many POEN it records for the receipt and issue of goods, for tasks and for the use of equipment, and the Foundation neither determines nor approves that; the amounts in exchange are set by the users themselves.

3. Collective decision-making. At the level of the cooperative, cooperative members already decide through its organs. At the level of the system the rules are established in the first phase by the Management Board, and in the second the Kolo keepers with activated ZRNO vote, by square root and with delegation. Users and members without confirmation, on whom the rules also act, do not vote, but the route to a vote is open to everyone: confirmation in the chain and inscription of ZRNO from one's own contribution. Proposals, the course of voting and the register of decisions are available to users.

4. Monitoring. The Protocol records every action; Kolo keepers supervise confirmations and introductions and confirm the completion of operational contribution; the ledger is available to participants in pseudonymous form. The supervisors are themselves users. The work of the Foundation's administrator team is supervised by the Management Board, and actions in the panel are recorded in the audit log.

5. Graduated sanctions. A false confirmation is annulled, and the one who gave it is answerable for it; suspension and exclusion are imposed under the procedure in the Terms of Use, with a right of complaint.

6. Conflict resolution. A dispute between users is resolved as close as possible to the place where it arose: where there is a cooperative, it helps, through its organs, to resolve the dispute amicably; while there is none, the user can ask the Foundation for help in amicable resolution. Neither the cooperative nor the Foundation makes a decision that binds the ledger, nor do they become a party to the exchange, and the right to go to court remains. Against decisions of the Foundation and against a record in the ledger the user submits a complaint to the Foundation.

7. Recognised right to self-organisation. The Foundation is registered under Serbian law, and the international framework (Appendix A) supports such forms of organisation.

8. Nested layers. Users and farms within the cooperative in their own place, the Upper Kolo at the level of the system and the Foundation as the legal instrument form layers with delimited competences; expansion to new regions is provided for with one Protocol. Ostrom calls for nested levels of decision-making, not more ledgers: one ledger with decision-making at the level of the cooperative and at the level of the system is consistent with that.

The architecture is designed to respond to all eight principles. Cooperatives carry the principles that call for proximity (rules adapted to the place, collective decision-making and conflict resolution) at the level of the place, and the system carries boundaries, monitoring and nesting. The weakest is collective choice at the level of the whole system in the first phase, while the rules are established by the Management Board. Whether the design works as intended is an empirical question.
